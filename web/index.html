Before

"Can I serve the strawberry yogurt cups as the meat alternate at snack? The label says 17 grams."

⬣wrong since October 2025

"Yes. Yogurt credits as a meat alternate and 17 grams is under the 23 gram limit." Confident, and the meal is disallowed at review. Followed for a month, that one answer is $194.04 not paid for Rosa's nine children, at this year's snack rate.

After, same agent, same question

⬣contradicts a rule Yogurt is creditable as a meat alternate only if it contains no more than 12 grams of added sugars per 6 ounces.

It rewrites, then asks the one fact the rule turns on: "Is the 17 grams added sugars or total sugars?"

■proved She reads 9 g added. The receipt is filed with the meal for the sponsor.

The first thing the tool caught was us: pointed at Tally's own rulebook, it found the yogurt limit in rules/cacfp_rules.json had expired a year earlier. On 30 questions USDA has answered, the same model as a plain assistant served 9 wrong answers; Tally with the tool served 1, and asked for the missing fact on 12. The paperwork happens at nine at night, and that is why it goes wrong.

A licensed family child care provider looks after four to twelve children in her own home. To be paid for the meals she serves, she must log every meal for every child by component under the federal food program. Since 2024 she must also document actual daily attendance for every subsidised child. She does all of it after the last child goes home.

52% gone Licensed homes

Licensed family child care homes fell by half between 2005 and 2017, more than 97,000 of them. Providers serving subsidised children fell 51 percent.

Almost 80 percent of state agencies name burdensome paperwork as the top barrier to food program participation.

Rosa runs a licensed home in Texas with nine children on a normal day and twelve after school. Last month a snack was rejected because the log showed one component. There were two. She forgot to write the second one down at twenty to ten at night.

On 19 August 2026 the National CACFP Association published a report on this, called Stretched Thin: The Administrative Burden of the CACFP in Family Child Care Homes. Reference 4 Tally is built for the problem that report describes.

Her hands are full. So there is nothing to type.

Say who is here

"Maya's here, Leo's here, Ava's mom said she's sick, Mateo's coming at eight." Attendance, the subsidy check and the ratio for the rest of the day, from one sentence.

Photograph the plate

The agent names the foods, maps them to components, and checks the meal pattern for every age group at the table. It never estimates portions.

Fix it while it is on the table

"Add milk and this breakfast qualifies." Not a rejection next month, when nothing can be done about it.

Approve, do not type

In the evening the notes home are drafted from what she already said, the claim builds itself, and she gets at most two questions.

When it is not sure, it asks

A pale liquid in a bowl could be milk or water, and the two are treated differently. Below its confidence threshold the agent leaves the item out of the record and asks one question, because guessing a component into compliance would create a false claim.

Allergies are checked before anything is written

Every food on the plate is checked against every present child's allergies first. In the demo it catches peanut butter in a bowl of porridge, on a real photograph, without anyone having planned it.

Three things a child care app does not do.

It works without hands

A camera and a voice. Two buttons for the moments a hand is free, and everything else spoken. Existing tools are forms, and forms get filled at nine at night, which is exactly when the mistakes happen.

It checks the rule before the meal ends

Reimbursability is decided at the table, with the smallest fix named out loud, rather than discovered at claim time when nothing can be done. Every verdict records the rule version it was decided under.

It uses the half of food vision that works

Models are good at naming foods and poor at estimating grams. The food program only needs components. So Tally identifies components, refuses to estimate portions, and says so plainly rather than quietly overreaching.

What about KidKare and the others?

ToolWhat it does wellWhat it does not do
KidKare by Minute Menu The food program standard, used by tens of thousands of homes, with thorough claim edit checks. Every meal is typed into a form, and its checks run at claim time, when a missing component can no longer be added.
brightwheel, Playground, Procare Run centres well: billing, enrolment, ratio tracking, CACFP menus. Assume somebody at a desk. A provider with a toddler on each hip is not that person.
Paper and a binder What many home providers actually use. Everything. This is the binder, filled in by a photograph and a sentence.

Tally exports in a format a sponsor already using KidKare can accept. That is deliberate: the aim is that her records are usable tonight, not that she switches systems.

Two shapes, because the day and the evening are different problems.

During the day the provider is standing there waiting, so the work is request driven and shallow: one specialist agent called as a tool. In the evening it is a fixed pipeline whose steps feed each other, so it is a Strands Graph.

The API.

The endpoint worth integrating is one line: send a photograph, get a compliant meal record. Generate a key and call it yourself, from here or from your terminal.

Call it

curl -X POST http://localhost:8001/api/meals \
  -H "x-api-key: YOUR_KEY" \
  -F "photo=@lunch.jpg" -F "meal_type=lunch"

# -> components, reimbursable, smallest_fix,
#    rule_version, questions

Use the key from the button above in place of YOUR_KEY, or leave the header off entirely. Full schema at /docs. The rules themselves are at /api/rules, so a sponsor can check them against the USDA tables.

Prove an answer

curl -X POST http://localhost:8001/api/prove \
  -H "x-api-key: YOUR_KEY" \
  -H "content-type: application/json" \
  -d '{"question": "Can I serve this yogurt as the meat alternate?",
       "facts": "The label says 17 g of added sugars per 6 oz.",
       "answer": "Yes, 17 g is under the 23 g limit."}'

# -> verdict: INVALID
#    rules: ["Yogurt is creditable as a meat alternate only
#             if it contains no more than 12 grams of added
#             sugars per 6 ounces."]

Put it in front of your own agent's rule answers. The reply is a proof by Amazon Bedrock Automated Reasoning against 7 CFR 226.20, not a second model's opinion. The same tool is on the MCP server as prove_answer, and as a standalone AWS Lambda in lambda/ for agents that are not Tally. Every receipt is filed in Amazon S3 with Object Lock, so a sponsor can read it three years on and nobody can have changed it.

Or run the whole thing yourself

git clone <repo> && cd tally
uv venv && uv pip install -e ".[dev]"
python -m tally.data.fetch_plates
python -m tally.data.make_plates
python -m tally.data.generate
uvicorn tally.api:app --port 8001

Then open http://localhost:8001. AWS credentials with Amazon Bedrock access are needed for the vision step.

POST /api/askAsk the Day agent a rule question, with the tool on or off.

What it will never do.

  • It never photographs children. The camera frames the plate. No picture of a child is stored.
  • It never submits a claim by itself. The provider reviews the month and presses send.
  • It never guesses a component into compliance. Below its confidence threshold it leaves the item out and asks.
  • It never estimates portions or calories. It identifies components, which is what the food program requires.
  • It never asks more than two questions a day outside a safety alert or a meal that can still be fixed. Enforced in code.
  • It never invents a rule. If a state's ratio table is not in the rules file, it refuses rather than guessing, because a wrong limit is worse than no answer.
